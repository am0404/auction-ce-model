# AUCTION_LAYER.md

What the auction layer does, what it deliberately does not, and why the answers
it gives are ranges.

> **Everything runnable in this document uses fabricated inputs.** The player
> pool, the projections and the acquisition costs in every example are
> generated by a formula. No output here describes a real player, and none of
> it is a market price.

---

## 1. Why a best alternative is necessary

A player's worth to you is not a property of the player. It is the difference
between the best roster you can reach *with* him and the best roster you can
reach *without* him and with his price back in your budget:

```
CE(best completed roster after buying X for $p)
  minus
CE(best completed roster after passing on X and keeping the $p)
```

Neither of those rosters exists until something searches for it. That is the
whole reason this layer exists: you cannot price a player without knowing what
else the money buys.

Two consequences follow immediately.

**The pass branch has to remove him from your alternatives.** Otherwise the
search happily re-buys him in the branch where you declined him, the two
branches converge, and every player prices at zero.

**Who gets him changes your answer.** Passing to the team chasing you and
passing to a team with no roster room are different futures, even though your
own roster is identical in both. The destination is an explicit input here, and
a test fails if it stops reaching the simulation.

## 2. Why dollars-per-point and dollars-per-CE do not work

Both are attempts to turn a valuation into a multiplication, and both break on
the same fact: **the budget is part of the question.**

A dollars-per-point conversion assumes a point is worth the same wherever it
lands. It is not. A point added to a player who cannot start is worth nothing;
the same point on the last starting flex slot is worth something; the same
point again on a bench player who covers three bye weeks is worth something
different. The 54-cell experiment measures exactly this: moving the same
replacement into the 15th roster spot has no measurable CE effect, while moving
him into the last non-QB starting slot resolves in all 54 scenarios.

A dollars-per-CE conversion fails for a subtler reason. At $18 you can still
afford some other player; at $19 you cannot. The best alternative is a
*different roster* at every price, so the comparison is a different question at
each one. That is why the reservation search actually searches the price domain
instead of dividing one CE difference by a constant.

**Which direction a jump can go is not symmetric.** With fixed acquisition
costs and a correctly optimised completion, every roster affordable after
paying `p + 1` was also affordable at `p`, so the buy branch's attainable
maximum is non-increasing in price; and the pass branch does not depend on our
price at all when the destination and the rival's price are fixed. A player
becoming unaffordable can therefore produce a genuine **downward** step. It can
never make the true optimum *improve* because we paid more.

An earlier version of this document and of the code described an upward step as
"real economics: a dollar here put somebody there out of reach". That was
wrong. An observed increase in delta CE is Monte Carlo noise, instability in
the bounded candidate search, instability in the CE selection, or a bug — and
the violation report now classifies it as one of those four rather than
excusing it.

## 3. Three prices, kept apart

| | What it is | Built here? |
|---|---|---|
| **CE reservation price** | The greatest price at which acquiring the player is at least as good as the modelled best alternative | **Yes** |
| **Expected clearing price** | What the room will actually pay | No, and not predictable from anything in this package |
| **Tactical winning bid** | What to bid, given nomination order, who else can still bid, and how their budgets are committed | No. The *state* such logic needs is built; the logic is not |

Nothing in this package may be presented as an opening maximum bid or a
recommended bid. A test walks every command's output looking for those phrases
and fails on any that is not inside an explicit denial.

## 4. Why values are scenario ranges

Four assumptions in the underlying model are unresolved: how the projection
source treats health, how much weekly variance is knowable before lineup lock,
how much persistent season-level uncertainty exists, and how fast a manager
learns a latent change. `ceauction.realdata.scenarios` enumerates the full
2 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 54-cell cross-product, and no cell is correct.

The committed experiment (`docs/example_slot_swap_54cell.txt`, 16,000 seasons
per arm, 23 minutes) ran one roster's two marginal slots through all 54:

| Band | Range | Width | Cells resolved |
|---|---|---:|---|
| marginal starter slot | `[-0.02038, -0.00331]` | 0.01706 | **54 of 54** |
| bench slot (15th man) | `[-0.00250, +0.00781]` | 0.01031 | 15 of 54 |
| adjacent alternative | `[-0.00550, +0.00331]` | 0.00881 | 15 of 54 |

Two findings, pointing different ways.

**Direction is robust where it matters.** Downgrading the last non-QB starting
slot costs championship equity in every single cell, with every cell's own
interval excluding zero. That a starting slot beats replacement does not depend
on any unresolved assumption.

**Magnitude is not.** The same effect ranges from −0.0033 to −0.0204 across the
grid, a factor of six. The band is 1.9× the CE gap between two adjacent players
at the bench slot and 3.1× at the starter slot, so an ordering of *neighbouring*
players is not supported by this model as it stands.

`availability_interpretation` is the dominant assumption in all three bands —
the same answer the calibration audit reached from a different direction.

**This does not gate the solver.** Opportunity cost exists in every cell:
passing on a player still means spending the money on someone. What the band
governs is *presentation* — scenario-dependent ranges with the dominant
assumption named, rather than points.

And it is two comparisons on one roster. It does not establish that every
ranking on the board is robust.

## 5. How individual opponent budgets enter

`AuctionState` holds every owner's own budget, roster and open slots, and
derives four different things from them:

* **reserve** — a dollar held for each open slot;
* **discretionary** — budget above that floor, the honest measure of remaining
  power. Two owners with the same cash and different roster room do not have
  the same room;
* **max bid** — `budget_remaining − (open_slots − 1)`, which is discretionary
  plus one. An owner with a full roster has a maximum of zero however much cash
  he holds;
* **feasibility** — whether a legal completion survives a purchase.

`bid_capacity(amount)` reports who *may* legally take the next dollar and, for
everyone else, which constraint stops them. It distinguishes an owner who wants
a player but cannot legally bid, one who can bid exactly one more dollar, one
with enough left to control the endgame, and one whose roster capacity makes the
player irrelevant. Reducing the room to "dollars left in the league" erases all
four.

Who *would* bid is not modelled. That is a behavioural question and inventing an
answer here would turn a stated assumption into an apparent finding.

## 6. How pass destination affects CE

`PassDestination.unavailable()` takes the candidate off the board. Nobody has
him; we keep our money.

`PassDestination.to_rival(owner, price)` awards him to one named opponent, who
pays, loses a roster slot, and re-completes his roster from what is left — so
the money he spent is money he no longer has for anyone else. He completes
*first*, because he has just won the player, which is the sequence the auction
actually has.

A rival with no open roster slot cannot receive the candidate, and the
comparison is refused rather than routed around. Silently displacing a player he
already owns would not represent an auction purchase.

## 7. How aggregate roster construction is preserved

The six non-QB starting slots interact. Three modest receivers healthy in
different weeks can beat two good ones and a hole; a fourth running back who can
never start is worth almost nothing however good he is. A greedy walk cannot see
any of that because it commits before it knows what it is committing to.

So the completion search is two-stage:

**Stage 1** is a beam over the board with take/skip at each player, ranked by
the *best legal eight* the state can field — not by the sum of projections,
which rates a sixth quarterback at his full value although he can never start.
The bound added to that is admissible: it grants the remaining slots the best
players of every position still on the board, and best-eight is monotone under
adding players.

The beam also keeps a slice of each **spending level**. Best-legal-eight
saturates once eight decent starters exist, so ranking on it alone collapses the
beam onto the cheapest route to those eight and an expensive, deeper roster
never survives to be compared. Bench depth is real and that scoring cannot see
it, so rather than invent a weight for it, the beam carries every spending level
and lets the availability proxy decide.

**Stage 2** re-ranks the survivors with `ceauction.auction.proxy`, which draws
the real bye and injury process and picks lineups with the real slot rules.

**Stage 3 chooses by championship equity**, simulating the finalists against a
fixed cast under common random numbers. **Stage 4 reports the winner on an
independent holdout sample** at an unrelated seed. Those last two are separate
on purpose: the maximum of several noisy estimates is biased upward, because
the winner won partly because its sample was kind to it, and quoting that same
sample would carry the bias into every price and every reservation frontier
built on it. Selection and evaluation counts are reported separately, and
constructing settings whose two seeds are equal is refused.

Finalists are diversified the same way: a runner-up that differs from the leader
by one interchangeable bench player answers nothing.

**A correction worth recording.** An earlier version ran both branches of a
buy/pass comparison with CE evaluation switched off, so each picked whichever
roster its *expected-points* proxy liked and only then simulated that single
roster. What it computed was `CE(proxy-selected completion)` while claiming
`CE(best modelled completion)`. Both are now CE-selected, and every result
states which rule chose it via `selection_basis`. A controlled fixture in
`tests/test_auction_audit_fixes.py` makes the two rules disagree — an underdog
whose fifteenth slot is a weekly starter, choosing between more points at
ordinary spread and fewer points at very wide spread — and the two paths pick
different players on it.

**Known limits.** The beam is a bounded search and is labelled heuristic
everywhere. The proxy is expected points, not equity, and is never called value.
The candidate pool is a real cut — a player below it cannot be chosen — and its
size is reported. The other eleven rosters are held fixed, which answers "which
completion is best against *this* league" and not "against a league still
drafting".

## 8. How QB3 insurance is represented

There is no QB3 premium and no rule that a team needs two quarterbacks. The
league has no quarterback maximum, and the superflex accepts a running back, so
a third quarterback earns his place only through:

* injury protection for the two ahead of him,
* bye coverage,
* and whatever margin a quarterback has over the skill player who would
  otherwise take the superflex.

All three come out of the simulation. Tests show the same third quarterback
gaining value when the two ahead of him get riskier, being worth almost nothing
when nobody gets hurt, and winning in one roster context while losing in
another. A grep-level test fails if any positional-premium constant is ever
added to the source.

## 9. How committee handcuffs are represented

A backup does not inherit his starter's job. The computational input is the
*conditional role change* — `Contingency(on_player_id, bonus)` — so:

| Shape | How it is expressed |
|---|---|
| direct handcuff | large bonus on one starter |
| partial handcuff | smaller bonus |
| committee | several backups, each with their own share, on the same starter |
| ambiguous backfield | near-zero bonus |
| standalone backup | weekly value in `base_mean`, independent of the bonus |

"Handcuff" is a label for humans. Tests confirm a committee back's projection
rises by exactly his share and never to the starter's level, and that four
backups with identical standalone projections order by CE the way their
conditional roles do.

One result is worth stating because it is not what a handcuff premium would
predict. The same backup came out worth **+0.0275** to the owner of his starter
and **+0.0295** to an owner who does not hold that starter. That is not a
defect. Holding the pair is insurance — the backup is up precisely when the
starter is gone — which narrows the spread of weekly scores without adding much
to their mean, while holding only the backup collects those weeks and lets
someone else absorb the absence. In a winner-take-all format a narrower spread
is not automatically better. The sign was free to come out either way because
nothing imposes one.

**Real handcuff mappings do not exist in this repository.** Every backfield in
the tests is fabricated, and nothing here invents a real depth chart.

## 10. Cost-source requirements

A completion search needs an assumed price for every player it might buy, and
this repository does not have this league's prices. The nearest thing available
is a 10-team half-PPR market file, which prices a different scarcity, a
different roster shape and a different superflex rule.

So provenance is a required field:

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `REAL` | observed in this league, this format, this season |
| `TRANSFORMED` | derived from a different format, transform named. **Still not this league's prices** |
| `PROVISIONAL` | a placeholder a human chose |
| `FABRICATED` | invented for a test |

Each level carries a disclaimer any report using the book must print. Merging a
weaker book downgrades the whole book — one fabricated price in a real book does
not leave a real book. **A missing cost raises**; defaulting an unpriced star to
$1 would tell the search he is free and it would take every one of them. A
fallback exists but has to be passed explicitly, which is the caller stating on
the record what an unpriced player is assumed to cost.

Acquisition cost and on-field performance live in separate objects, because they
are different quantities from different sources with different error.

## 11. Monte Carlo and search limitations

**Monte Carlo.** Every CE number is an estimate. Paired common random numbers
make the two arms of a comparison share seasons, so a player on both rosters
contributes an exact zero to the difference and the interval is as tight as the
design allows. A difference whose interval contains zero is reported as
unresolved and never ordered on noise. Finalist completions that cannot be
separated come back as a co-best *set*.

**Every interval reported is POINTWISE 95%, not a simultaneous band.** A run
covering many prices and many scenarios makes many such statements at once, and
the probability that at least one of them is wrong grows with their number — so
the robust/permissive range as a whole does **not** carry 95% joint coverage. A
proper simultaneous procedure is **not implemented**. A conservative
Bonferroni-adjusted alternative is available via
`ReservationResult.bonferroni_intervals()`; it is valid but wide, because it
ignores the heavy positive dependence between neighbouring prices that share
most of their rosters and all of their random numbers.

**A sparse price ladder brackets a frontier; it does not name one.** Testing
$20 and then $50 says the frontier is somewhere in $20–$49, not that it is $20.
Results report the *highest tested favorable price*, the next tested
unfavorable price, every untested interval, and whether the ladder was
exhaustive. An integer frontier is reported only when every relevant price was
actually evaluated. `--refine` goes and evaluates every integer in the
transition gap; it does **not** bisect, because bisection would assume the
monotonicity the violation check exists to test.

**Search.** The beam is heuristic. Where a board is small enough, exhaustive
enumeration is available as a testing oracle and the beam is checked against it,
but on a real board it is not a proof of optimality, and the difference reported
by a buy/pass comparison is between the best rosters *this search found*.

## 12. Exact commands

```bash
# room state
ce-lab auction validate
ce-lab auction room --next-bid 20

# best alternative for one owner
ce-lab auction complete --sims 3000 --beam-width 120 --candidate-pool 45 --finalists 4

# buy versus pass, both destinations
ce-lab auction buy-pass --price 20 --sims 4000 --scenario fh-f000-s000-w1
ce-lab auction buy-pass --price 20 --pass-to Owner02 --pass-price 22 --sims 4000

# CE reservation range over a price ladder and three scenarios
ce-lab auction reservation --prices 1 5 10 20 35 50 75 99 --sims 3000 \
    --beam-width 100 --candidate-pool 40 --finalists 3 \
    --scenarios fh-f000-s000-w1 aa-f000-s000-w1 fh-f050-s020-n0

# runtime estimate without running anything
ce-lab auction reservation --prices 1 20 --estimate-only

# the committed 54-cell experiment (needs a real contract; ~23 minutes)
ce-lab auction slot-swap --contract local_data/real_player_contract_v1.json \
    --limit 300 --sims 16000
```

Committed outputs live in `docs/examples/` and `docs/example_slot_swap_54cell.txt`.

## 13. Measured runtimes

Fabricated demo auction, 12 owners, 310-player pool, 3,000 seasons per arm.

| Operation | Median |
|---|---:|
| state validation | 0.001s |
| legal maxima for all 12 owners | <0.001s |
| fingerprint | <0.001s |
| completion search, proxy only | 0.43s |
| state fingerprint (hashes every PlayerSpec field) | 0.003s |
| completion search + CE selection + holdout (4 finalists) | 4.75s |
| buy/pass, unavailable | 11.2s |
| buy/pass, named rival (rival re-completes) | 18.3s |
| reservation, 1 scenario × 6 prices | 71.0s |
| 54-cell slot swap, 16,000 seasons | 23 min |

**Projected:** a full 54-scenario × 12-price reservation range is about
**two hours**. That is an offline workflow, and it is described as one.

Before the audit a buy/pass comparison took 2.55s, because each branch stopped
at its proxy and simulated one roster. Evaluating finalists by equity in both
branches, optimising the rival's continuation for his own equity, and reporting
on an independent holdout costs about 4.4×. That is the price of computing the
quantity the documentation always claimed, and the old number should not be
quoted as though the two measured the same thing.

**Nothing here is live-capable.** A single buy/pass comparison is 2.5 seconds on
fabricated data; a reservation range is minutes. A 10-second bid timer would
need a precomputed table, not this search. Saying otherwise on the strength of a
synthetic benchmark would be exactly the error this document exists to avoid.

## 14. What this layer does not do

No dollar values for real players. No opening bids. No live bids. No auction
inflation. No prediction of who will bid or what anything will clear for. No
multi-owner simultaneous completion — the other eleven rosters are held fixed.
No in-season waivers, trades, or post-draft replacement, which means a
quarterback's real scarcity cannot yet be fully priced. No simultaneous
confidence band over prices and scenarios.

## 15. Terms this document keeps apart

| Term | Means |
|---|---|
| **proxy-selected completion** | chosen by expected weekly starting points. Cheap, and not equity |
| **CE-selected completion** | chosen by simulated championship equity on a selection sample |
| **holdout equity** | the chosen roster's equity on an independent sample. The number to quote |
| **highest tested favorable price** | the greatest price that was *evaluated* and came back favorable |
| **reservation bracket** | at least X, below Y. Where the frontier is, when the ladder left a gap |
| **exact integer reservation frontier** | a single price, reported only when every relevant integer was evaluated |
| **pointwise uncertainty** | a 95% interval for one price in one scenario |
| **joint scenario uncertainty** | coverage across all prices and scenarios at once. **Not implemented** |
